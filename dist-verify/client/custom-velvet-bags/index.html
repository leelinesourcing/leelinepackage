Five Ways to Put a Mark on Velvet

Velvet is the least cooperative surface in this workshop. The pile drinks ink, shrugs off adhesive and blurs a fine line, which puts thread at the top of the list rather than the bottom.

  • Embroidery

    Thread laid into the pile. The only route that still reads after a wash, and the route that holds a crest or a monogram at a size the others lose.

  • Foil stamping

    A metal layer pressed into the pile under heat. On a flat crushed velvet it lands sharp and mirror-bright; on a deep pile it sinks and softens instead of flattening.

  • Screen print

    Ink pushed through a mesh onto the face. Bold shapes only, and it sits on top of the cloth: a print on a pouch that gets handled will wear before the cloth does.

  • Deboss

    The pile pressed down with no ink and no foil. A mark that cannot be read from a distance and cannot be missed in the hand, which suits a house that does not shout.

  • Woven or leather label

    A separate label stitched at the seam. Fine type and two colors both survive here, at the price of another component to make and another thing to sew on.

Detail that will not hold at the size asked for is caught on the drawing, not on a sample — the drawing comes back with the size it needs.

Before the product goes in

Four Things the Cloth Is Tested Against

Cloth that marks what it wraps is worse than no cloth at all. These four are decided before the run starts, not discovered in a customer’s hand.

  • Grade 4–5

    Dry rub fastness

    A black pouch against a white garment is the first question a retail buyer asks, and the grade is the answer. ISO 105-X12, tested on the finished dyed cloth, not on a swatch of yarn.

  • Azo-free

    Dye to REACH Annex XVII

    A restricted amine is not something a pouch should carry into a customer’s home. The dye declaration travels with the order, which matters for anything a child will pick up.

  • Two passes

    Brushed, then vacuumed

    Velvet sheds onto glass, lenses and polished metal. The cloth is worked against its own pile and vacuumed before packing, which is what opens a box clean.

  • Under 2 %

    Shrinkage at 40 °C

    The pouches that come back into circulation are the ones that get washed. Cloth that survives a hand wash at its original size is cloth a customer keeps.

Why the Second Order Happens

Two things stop a reorder: packaging the customer throws away, and a second run that does not match the first. Both are decided in the first run.

  • A box is opened once and binned

    What we do

    Build a pouch worth keeping: a tarnish-cloth lining so silver comes out the colour it went in, a seam that survives a wash, and a size that fits a handbag rather than a shelf.

    What that gets you

    The mark keeps working long after the sale — out of a drawer, a suitcase and a gym bag for years, at no further cost per impression.

  • Run two never matches run one

    What we do

    Dye to the chip signed off on the first run rather than to a screen, hold the run to a tolerance of ΔE 1.5 against it, and dye a reorder placed inside six months in the same bath.

    What that gets you

    Two batches read as one colour on a shelf, so a reorder never looks like a different product beside the stock already there.

Before the swatches go out

What Buyers Ask Before the First Order

None of these six is answered anywhere above. If something here is holding an order up, ask it directly and it will be answered inside a day.

  1. 01
    What size pouch holds a necklace and its matching earrings?

    Measure the longest dimension of the piece and allow 15 mm of clearance on every side. A 45 cm chain doubled over sits in a 4 × 6 in pouch, and studs disappear in anything above 3 × 4 in, so a set usually reads as one 4 × 6 and two 3 × 4 rather than one bag for the lot. Inches are enough to quote from.

  2. 02
    Which lining belongs inside a silver pouch, and which inside a gift pouch?

    Tarnish cloth where the piece is going to be stored, because silver and gold dull on the inside of the cloth rather than on the outside air. Satin where the pouch is presentation and the piece moves to a box the same evening. A lining is a second component sewn into the same seam, so it moves the sewing line of the quote rather than the cloth line.

  3. 03
    What do I send so the artwork can be used as it is?

    A vector outline rather than a picture, with the strokes already at the width the route needs: 0.5 mm for a foil stamp or a deboss, 2 mm for embroidery. A raster file is redrawn here first, and the redraw comes back as a line drawing to approve before anything is priced. Pantone references rather than screen colors for anything printed.

  4. 04
    How do I choose between a cord, a zip and a frame clasp?

    A cord or a ribbon where the pouch is opened often and closed with one pull, and where nothing inside is small enough to fall out. A zip where the pouch becomes a case and travels in a handbag, because the contents are held even upside down. A frame clasp where the bag is carried by the hand instead of kept in a drawer — on that build the closure is the point rather than an addition.

  5. 05
    Can one run carry more than one size or color?

    Yes, and the 500-unit minimum applies to the order rather than to each variant, so a first run can be three sizes in two colors. Weight and packing follow the total count rather than the number of variants. A second mark is the one change that arrives with its own setup.

  6. 06
    How is the price built?

    Four lines: the cloth, the sewing and the closure, the mark, and the packing. Cloth and sewing are per unit and fall as the quantity rises. The mark is the line a first order carries and a reorder does not, because an embroidery card, a screen or a stamping die is made once and filed against your pattern. Packing follows how the pouches leave — loose, or folded into a kit.
