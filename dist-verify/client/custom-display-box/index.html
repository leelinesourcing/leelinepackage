Maker, not trader

Three Things You Get From a Maker, Not a Trader

A trading company quotes the work and then looks for a plant to run it. We are the plant. These three are what that changes for you, whatever the volume.

  • Checked Three Times, Not Once

    Mill certificate, mid-run, strike-off

    Board grade and flute are read against the mill certificate on arrival, the unit is checked against the shelf envelope mid-run, and the finish is checked against the signed strike-off before the lot is packed.

  • The Rate Drops Before You Scale

    Breaks at 1,000 / 5,000 / 10,000

    Die and print setup are listed separately in the quote, and the unit rate steps down at 1,000, 5,000 and 10,000. A reorder skips those setup lines, which is where the second run gets cheaper.

  • One Floor, No Handoffs

    Sheet, press, die-cutter, gluer

    Sheet stock, the press, the die-cutter and the folder-gluer stand on the same floor, so a unit is printed, cut and glued without being palletized between suppliers. A fault is found where it happened, not at the end of a chain.

Print and coating

Four Coatings, Chosen by Where the Unit Stands

A counter tray is handled by hundreds of people a week and a floor stand is not. That difference settles the coating far more often than the artwork does. Each panel below is the same board under a different finish.

  • Aqueous (AQ)

    Water-based, food-safe

    Cures fast and recycles with the board. The default on food and beverage units, because it clears food-contact review without a film going onto the print.

    Sheen

  • Lamination

    Gloss, silk or matte film

    Takes the scuffing a counter unit collects and holds color on a display that stands under store lighting for a season. Applied spot over a logo when the budget will not carry the whole panel.

    Sheen

  • Soft-Touch

    Velvety, fingerprint-resistant

    For beauty and fragrance, where the first thing a customer does is put a hand on the unit. It also hides the handling marks that show up quickly on a plain matte finish.

    Sheen

  • UV Coating

    Glass-like gloss, instant cure

    The hardest surface of the four and the brightest color underneath it. Spot UV over a matte base is the usual build when a single part of the artwork has to carry the message.

    Sheen

Questions

What Buyers Ask Before a Display Order

01Will the unit fit the shelf my retailer specifies?

Not without the retailer’s fixture numbers. Those settle the dieline, and a stock tray size is the one thing that reliably fails a planogram check. Where the plan is not available yet, we build to a common grocery, convenience or club envelope and mark every assumption on the drawing, so you have something concrete to take back to the buyer.

02What does a floor stand actually hold?

Its rating, and nothing past it. The rating is not a default — a heavier product is quoted against a stronger board or an added base pad, so the figure you are given is the figure that unit holds, and it is printed on the elevation that comes with the quote.

03Who assembles the display — the plant or the store?

Both, and the split is fixed at the quote stage rather than left to the store. Shelf-ready trays leave filled and sealed, so nothing gets built on site at all. Floor stands and counter units are the only ones anyone assembles.

04What changes for me on a second run?

Your artwork, and only if the artwork moved. Nothing else is set up twice, and there is no sample round to sign off again. Where a color did change, the new ink is read beside a unit held back from the original run, so the second printing matches the first on the shelf.

05What do you need from me to price a display?

Four things: a size, the product going into it, the quantity, and the date it has to be in store. Product weight and a photo settle the insert and the board grade. Everything else in the quote follows from those four.

06How do the units ship, and what does freight cost?

Freight is charged on a pallet of collapsed board, not on assembled display volume, and that is usually the difference between a landed cost that works and one that does not. Multi-destination orders ship as separate consignments drawn from a single production lot, so a split delivery does not become a second order.
