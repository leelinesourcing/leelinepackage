The Surface Decides the Adhesive

Nearly every label we print sticks to one of six surfaces. Get the pairing wrong and it curls, clouds or slides off, which reads as a printing fault when it never was one.

What you're labelling→The adhesive it takes

  • On the surface

    Flat board

    Dry, flat and sealed, with no radius and no oil to work around.

    →Takes the adhesivePermanent acrylic

    Aggressive and immediate, which is why nothing lifts over time.

  • On the surface

    Curved bottle

    A face that has to follow the radius of the bottle.

    →Takes the adhesiveConformable, with an overlap

    Soft enough to bend with the curve instead of bridging across it.

  • On the surface

    Oily jar

    Oil at the shoulder of a jar crawls under an acrylic and lifts it.

    →Takes the adhesiveRubber-based, high tack

    Enough tack to grip through the film of oil.

  • On the surface

    Chilled and wet

    Milk tanks and cold rooms, where water sits between the label and the glass.

    →Takes the adhesiveWet-surface acrylic

    Pushes the water aside rather than sitting on top of it.

  • On the surface

    Frozen, −18 °C

    A standard adhesive goes hard below freezing and pops off.

    →Takes the adhesiveFreezer-grade

    Stays soft enough to hold through the freeze.

  • On the surface

    Recycled kraft

    Dusty, porous board that drinks adhesive.

    →Takes the adhesiveExtra-coverage coat

    A heavier coat, which is what keeps the corners down on day three.

Four Substrates, Four Ways a Label Fails

Each stock buys a property and pays for it in another. The failure named on each panel is what that stock exists to prevent.

  • BOPP film

    Sharpest print of the four

    Clear or white film with a glossy face, which is why it prints the sharpest of the four. Left on a sunlit shelf it hardens. It belongs indoors on a jar, not outdoors on a crate.

    Fails atGoes brittle in sunlight

  • Vinyl (PVC)

    Outdoors and in water

    Water resistant, UV resistant and soft enough to conform to a curve. The stock for anything that spends its life outside or below the waterline — the price is the trade.

    Fails atCosts the most

  • Metallic foil

    Returns the room light

    Refuses a laser scanner, and turns as the angle changes. It reads as the room rather than as a print, which is why a foil panel and a barcode belong on two different labels.

    Fails atWill not read as a barcode

  • Textured synthetic

    Tactile, and crack-free

    Paper-look, and it shrugs off water and bends around a tight radius without splitting, printed with a texture you can feel under a thumb. No pen marks it.

From file to roll

Five Stops, From Press File to Shipped Roll

Two of the five happen before a single label is printed, and both are the ones that decide whether the run is right.

  1. 01
    Day 0–1

    Artwork check

    The file is read before a die is quoted, and what comes back is a short list of anything that would have stopped the run. No charge for the check, and no die is drawn until the list is clear.

  2. 02
    Day 1–3

    Die and press proof

    Your outline is cut into the die, and the proof is printed on the stock you chose rather than on a generic sheet. Nothing goes on the press until that proof is signed.

  3. 03
    Day 3–7

    Sample roll

    A short run off the real die, with the cut, the peel and the color all tested by hand. Five to seven days from proof approval, and the sample is credited against the production order.

  4. 04
    Week 2–5

    The run

    Press time is booked at the proof, on the stock booked with it, at the gap and the register the drawing specifies. Four to six weeks door to door on a sea shipment, on a slot that was locked the day the proof was signed.

  5. 05
    On dispatch

    Inspection and pallet

    Each roll is checked for register and for cut depth before it is wound onto the core, shrink-wrapped and palleted with a packing sheet. Under 0.2% of what ships arrives unusable.

Six answers

Six Answers Before the Artwork Goes Out

The questions that come up between approving a proof and opening the first carton.

01Can you match the label we already buy from another printer?

Send a sample of the label being replaced, unstuck from the bottle or the carton. A physical piece carries the face, the cut depth and the adhesive in a way no file describes, and it settles what the replacement has to equal before anything is quoted. Where the current label has lifted is the part of the brief worth telling us about.

02How close will the color be to the brand palette?

A spot color goes on press as a mixed ink and is matched to a physical chip, not to a screen — which is the part buyers find hardest to accept, because each screen renders the same hex differently. A chip is signed before the run, so the color is argued about once and not at every reprint.

03What happens if the outline changes after the die is cut?

A change inside the outline — a legal line, an ingredient list, a price — is a change to the artwork alone and leaves the die untouched. Moving the outline itself needs a new blade, because the blade is cut to that line and cannot be edited.

04Several different labels on a single order — is that possible?

Yes. Each design is cut on its own die and wound onto its own roll, which means a mixed pallet arrives already sorted and nothing on the packing line has to be separated by hand.

05If the cut or the color is off when the roll arrives, what happens?

The signed proof is the reference, and a roll that does not match it is remade at our cost on the same tool — scheduled ahead of new work rather than behind it.

06Is the label recyclable, and what happens to the backing paper?

A paper face from FSC-certified stock with soy-based ink is recyclable in most streams. The release liner is silicone-coated and generally is not, which is a real limit on a sticker and worth knowing before a sustainability claim goes on the pack.
