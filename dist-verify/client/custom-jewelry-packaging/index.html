Three Ways a Piece Arrives Damaged

Sulfur in the air, a lid that shuts too low, and a well cut to the wrong size — all three settle the outcome long before anybody opens the box.

  • 01

    The lid leaves a mark on the stone

    The insert holds the piece below the line where lid meets base, and the sample is closed and reopened on the bench before the run starts.

    The stone arrives exactly as it left the bench

  • 02

    Silver goes dark in the box

    A sulfur-free layer is laid against the metal itself rather than over the adhesive behind it, and it is the layer we name on the spec sheet.

    Stock that still reads as new after a season in the case

  • 03

    The piece scuffs itself on the way

    Board is harder than metal, and a loose well is what lets the piece travel. Sized around the piece and the clasp, there is nothing for it to move against.

    A piece that comes out of the box unmarked, however far it travelled

Linings and Anti-Tarnish Protection

Silver dulls in a container weeks before a shop ever sees it, and it is the cloth in contact with the metal that decides the outcome. Four cloths, a single job each.

  • Velvet

    Rings and branded retail boxes

    A polished stone meets nothing abrasive.

  • Suede or microfiber

    Modern lines where shine is unwanted

    The chain comes out unmarked, on a face with no shine to it.

  • EVA or foam

    Transit and mixed-SKU trays

    Freight cannot move the piece, because the cavity was cut around it.

  • Satin over card

    Necklaces and certificates

    Under satin, a chain reads as deliberate.

In a sulfur-free lined box

The metal stays display-ready, and stock does not have to be polished or discounted before it reaches a cabinet.

In an unlined box

The darkening happens in transit and in the stockroom, which is why it usually arrives as a customer complaint rather than a packing note.

What the Outside Is Made Of

The outside is where the brand is read and where most of the cost sits. Four decisions, and each one is settled against the other three.

  • Rigidity comes from the board, which is what keeps a full tray from bowing.

  • A recycled sheet that still takes foil cleanly, for a range sold on what it leaves behind.

  • A grain that hides handling marks — that is what survives a counter better than a flat wrap.

  • Settled with the wrap, because each method bites differently into a texture — and the one that flakes is the one that gets sent back.

People Also Ask About Custom Jewelry Packaging

Six questions that come up before a brief is settled.

01What do you need to start a quote?

Less than most buyers expect. No artwork and no dieline are needed — those come back from us. What does help is the logo in vector form and the colour reference you print to, so what comes back matches what you sell.

02Can you copy a box we already use?

Send a physical sample, or photographs with the outside dimensions. Both the pattern and its cavity are rebuilt to match, and the files are kept against your reference.

03How is the price built?

Board and wrap, assembly, and the insert with its pouch. The mark sits outside those three as a separate line, because the die is cut once and kept — which is why a reorder reads lower than the first order.

04Do you need the jewelry itself?

It is the fastest route, and the piece comes back with the first sample. Where something cannot travel, the well depth is the measurement worth taking by hand.

05How do the boxes ship?

Flat where the structure folds and assembled where it does not, on pallets or in cartons. The carton count and the weight travel with the despatch papers; the receiving end is not guessing.

06How is the minimum counted?

Once per structure, per size and per wrap — that is the unit it is counted in, not a monthly volume. It is also what makes the first order expensive and the tenth not.
